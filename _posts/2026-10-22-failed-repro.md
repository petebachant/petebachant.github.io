Ever since starting my PhD back in 2012,
I was totally on board with the Open Science movement.
Every paper I published (barring commercial restrictions)
included open code, data, simulation configs, even computer aided design (CAD)
files.
And yet, throughout all those rounds of peer review,
not once did a referee actually try to run the code.

Maybe it was because the code didn't look easy to run,
or it wasn't all in one place,
or the work seemed simple enough that there was no risk that the
code didn't match the mathematical formulas and prose describing
the methods.
Whatever it was, the "supplementary materials"---which
I feel are actually part of a complete description of the methods,
not just supplementary---were
not checked, as far as I could tell.

Fast forward to 2025 and I'm working with a team at Caltech,
the University of Minnesota, and more,
to scale up an astronomical alert broker to accommodate
the much heavier data stream from the
Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST),
a survey that will facilitate countless discoveries in astrophysics
and astronomy.

We wrote a paper to announce this new broker,
[_BOOM_](https://github.com/boom-astro/boom),
which included a scaling test to show that indeed,
this new system could scale to meet the increased load much
better than the previous one.
I used this as an opportunity to "dogfood"
[Calkit](https://github.com/calkit/calkit) a bit
and integrate this scaling
test and its results with the writing,
which was done by most others on the team in Overleaf.

We were changing the code and iterating on how the test was
run dozens of times, and I didn't want the
["reproducibility anxiety"](https://docs.calkit.org/testimonials)
or tedium of manually copying and pasting figures and results into Overleaf.
I wanted to focus on getting the whole pipeline working cleanly,
correctly, and automatically, so my limited brainpower could be directed
towards its validity, not remembering what files need to be shuffled
around or what commands need to be run.
The final scaling enhancement factor was an output of the pipeline,
injected into the LaTeX source, so even that did not need a find-and-replace
when it inevitably changed as we iterated.

Calkit has a feature for automatically bundling and uploading
the entire project to an archival service like Figshare,
Zenodo, or our own CaltechDATA, then adding it as a reference (with a DOI)
to the project's BibTeX file (also synced with Overleaf),
so it can be cited in the paper.
However, this feature had a bug while creating our initial submission,
and to my surprise, one of the reviewers downloaded the project
and tried to run it, but certain critical files were excluded.
They tried some workarounds but ultimately gave up and let us know.

I fixed that bug and we made some adjustments to the benchmarking pipeline
to make it more realistic per the reviewers' recommendations.
The reviewer successfully ran (and even extended) the next release
of the full Calkit project, and the article was accepted for
[publication](https://doi.org/10.1088/1538-3873/ae6fef).

Looking back, we could probably have made it easier on ourselves
by not sharing a compendium that claimed to be
["single-button reproducible."]({% post_url 2025-10-17-single-button %})
Our software's scalability was not a groundbreaking scientific discovery.
The code and data simply being available is usually enough of a signal
that the authors deserve some trust.
That is changing, however, with more and more research output
being generated by AI.
I do hope to see standards emerge for "repro packs" or "calculation kits" used
to speed up end-to-end iteration during the research and as a
critical verification step before publication.
The "just trust me bro" policy is going to be unworkable.
